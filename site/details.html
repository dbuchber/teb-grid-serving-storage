Can home batteries and electric cars relieve the local grid around Steyr?

This page walks through our answer to the hackathon challenge step by step: the task, the terms we need, how we built the simulation, which assumptions we made, which data we used, what came out, and what we propose. All figures are results of a computer model built from open data, not measurements. As of 24 Oct 2026.

1 The task of the hackathon

The challenge “Grid-Friendly / Grid-Serving Storage” starts from two developments:

  • More and more batteries are installed. Battery prices are falling. Many households put a battery next to their rooftop solar system so they can use more of their own electricity.
  • The local power grid is reaching its limits. More solar systems feed in at midday; heat pumps and charging of electric cars add demand in the evening and in winter. Parts of the grid will be overloaded.

The task as set by the challenge: the company that runs the local grid wants to use the batteries of its customers as a simple, quick measure against overloads. Normally the owner decides how the battery runs. Only in exceptional situations and for a limited time may the grid company influence many batteries in one part of the grid at once. The team is to describe the processes and interfaces for this as a rough “interoperability profile” using the IES method.

We split this into three questions and answer them in order on this page:

  1. Where and whendoes the grid around Steyr get overloaded, today and in the future?
  2. Who can helpHome batteries, electric cars, or neither, and how many would it take?
  3. How to askHow does the grid company ask the batteries safely and fairly? This is the interoperability profile.

2 Terms used on this page

Every technical term on this page is explained here or in the step where it first appears.

The grid

Grid operator (DSO)
The company that runs the local power lines (distribution system operator). It does not sell electricity.
Substation
Large station that takes power from the high-voltage grid and passes it to the medium-voltage grid of a region. On the map and in tables substations are named “UW …” (German “Umspannwerk”).
Medium voltage (MV)
Lines between the substation and the transformer stations in the streets.
Transformer station
Small station in a neighbourhood that converts medium voltage to the low voltage used in homes. We often just say “transformer”. The Steyr region has 257 of them.
Low-voltage grid, feeder
The cables or overhead lines from a transformer to the houses. A transformer has several outgoing lines, called feeders.
Grid segment
One transformer together with all houses connected to it, or one of its feeders. The grid operator addresses its requests per segment.
kW, kWh
Kilowatt is power (how much at one moment), kilowatt-hour is energy (power over time). MW = 1,000 kW.
Reactive power, power factor, Q(U)
Part of the current only swings back and forth and does no useful work, but it still loads cables and transformers. The power factor is the share of useful power (0.97 means 3 % is lost to this). Q(U) is a rule in the Austrian connection rules: solar inverters adjust their reactive power to the local voltage.
kVA, rating
The size of a transformer, in kilovolt-amperes. For this page you can read 1 kVA as about 1 kW (Austrian rules set solar inverters to no reactive power by default; we check the effect separately). The rating is the power the transformer may carry continuously. For a few hours, especially when it is cold outside, it may carry more without harm; step 7 checks this as well.
Feed-in, demand
Feed-in: houses send power into the grid (solar at midday). Demand: houses draw power from the grid.
Bottleneck, overload
An hour in which the power through a transformer is above its rating. “Overload hours” counts these hours per year. A bottleneck is thermally critical if the transformer would also get too hot (step 7); only then is it urgent. A line overload is an hour in which the current in a cable section is above its rated current.
Curtailment
The grid operator limits a solar system so it feeds in less. The energy is lost.
Grid reinforcement
Replacing a transformer with a larger one or laying more cables. Expensive and slow.

The devices in the houses

PV, kWp
Photovoltaic (solar) system on the roof. kWp (kilowatt peak) is its size: the power under full sun.
Inverter
The device between solar panels or battery and the house wiring that converts direct current into household alternating current.
Box
Our proposed control unit next to the electricity meter: it receives the grid operator's requests, checks them and controls the battery (chapter 5).
Home battery
A battery in the house, usually installed with a PV system. We count it in units of 10 kWh energy and 5 kW power.
Self-consumption
How batteries run today: store the household's own solar surplus, use it in the evening. The share of solar power used at home is the self-consumption rate.
Heat pump
Electric heating that takes heat from the air or the ground. It needs the most power on cold days.
Electric car (EV), wallbox
EV = electric vehicle. The wallbox is its charger at home.
Smart charging
The grid operator may slow down wallboxes while the transformer is full; the car is still fully charged by 7 am.
V2G
Vehicle-to-grid: a parked car not only charges but also gives power back to the grid for a while.

The method

Model, simulation
A computer program that calculates, hour by hour, how much power flows through each transformer.
Synthetic
Generated by us from rules and open data because the real data is not public. Marked as such wherever it appears.
Mocked
Invented example data that only illustrates how a process works.
Scenario
An assumed future state: today, 2030, a moderate 2040 or the 2040 stress case, with more heat pumps, EVs and PV.
Weather year
The real weather of one past year that we run the simulation with: 2012 (cold spell), 2017, or 2023 (mild).
Median
The middle value: half of the transformers (or runs) are above it, half below.
Sensitivity
We change one assumption at a time and see how much a result moves. Large moves mark the assumptions that matter.

3 How we went about it, step by step

No grid operator publishes its real low-voltage grid, because it is critical infrastructure. So we rebuilt the grid of the Steyr region from open data and simulated it. Each step below says what we did, what we assumed and where the data comes from. A complete list follows in chapter 7.

Step 1 · Choose a region and find its transformers

We chose Steyr and the surrounding district Steyr-Land in Upper Austria: a town, suburbs and villages, so both strong urban and weak rural grids occur. We took the positions of the 257 transformer stations and of the substations from open map data. Transformers keep their OpenStreetMap identifier, for example “way/543897968”.

Assumption
Where the map data has no transformer for a group of houses, we place one synthetically (shown hollow on the map). Medium-voltage lines are drawn along roads for the map only; they are not simulated.
Data
OpenStreetMap (transformer stations, roads); substation capacities from ebUtilities.

Step 2 · Connect the buildings: a synthetic low-voltage grid

Every building from the official address register is assigned to the nearest transformer in a straight line (at most 600 m away). From there the cables follow the streets. We sized the transformers and feeders after three published Austrian field grids: 3–11 feeders per transformer, 2.8–4.6 kVA of transformer per building, cables in town, overhead lines in the countryside, and an own feeder for large PV systems.

Assumption
The grids are plausible, not exact. Transformer size = the larger of 3 kVA per residential building, 2 kVA per household and 0.7 kVA per kWp of today's PV, rounded up to a standard size. These are the low end of the three field grids, so our transformers tend to be small; the sensitivity in chapter 4 shows what larger ones change. Because today's PV is part of the sizing, today's feed-in bottlenecks are few by construction. Assigning buildings along the streets instead changes which transformer they belong to for about one in seven buildings (also shown in chapter 4). PV systems from 30 kWp get their own feeder. For the line results we built a second, more detailed synthetic grid with 432 stations and sized cable sections (chapter 4, lines).
Data
Austrian address register (BEV); reference grids of the DG DemoNet project (Eberstalzell, Littring, Köstendorf, final report 2015) and TU Wien reference feeders (2018).

Step 3 · Find the solar systems on the roofs

The BOKU University mapped the PV systems in Austria from aerial images taken 2021–23. For each roof orientation we compute the hourly output with PVGIS, the EU solar calculator. To see what was built since, we trained our own image-recognition model (the AI part of this project) on the BOKU outlines and ran it on the 2025 aerial images. Recall is the share of real systems the model finds, precision the share of its finds that are real:

Data

Step 4 · Give some houses a home battery

There is no public register of home batteries. We assign them by rule.

Assumption
Half of the households with PV and 20 % of farms and businesses with PV have a battery. Brands, ages and response behaviour of the batteries are mocked.
Data
None public; synthetic.

Step 5 · Add households, heat pumps and electric cars

Each household gets the Austrian standard load profile. Each building gets a heating demand from its size and age; a heat pump turns that into electricity, depending on the outdoor temperature. Electric cars arrive home at random times in the evening and charge what they used during the day; in the cold they use more.

The 2040 stress case replaces almost every oil and gas heating outside the district-heating areas with a heat pump, as climate neutrality in 2040 would require. Studies expect less: the Austrian Environment Agency projects heat pumps at over 25 % of heating capacity in 2040, TU Wien about a third of heated floor area by 2050; wood and district heating stay important in Upper Austria. The 2040 moderate case keeps the heat pumps at 35 % and is closer to these studies. Unless marked otherwise, “2040” on this page means the stress case: read it as an upper bound.

Assumption
Which buildings get a heat pump or an EV is random. Cars drive 12,000 km a year. Areas with district heating (Münichholz, Ennsleite, Resthof, Tabor) get no heat pumps. Heat pumps are never switched off by the grid operator. Building renovation until 2040 is not modelled.
Data
Standard load profile H0 (APCS); building shapes DORIS LoD1 (Land Upper Austria); heating demand per building type and age (TABULA), calibrated to Statistik Austria; heat-pump efficiency per temperature (when2heat).

Step 6 · Use real weather

Sun and temperature decide both midday feed-in and winter demand. We run three real years: 2012 with a cold spell, 2017, and the mild 2023. The spread between them shows how robust a result is.

Assumption
One temperature per transformer (1 km grid).
Data
GeoSphere Austria INCA, checked against the Wachtberg station near Steyr (difference ≤ 0.1 °C); PVGIS for the same years.

Step 7 · Simulate every hour of the year

For every transformer we add up, hour by hour for all 8,760 hours of a year, what its houses draw and feed in, and compare the result with the transformer's rating. Bottlenecks are rare events, often only a few hours a year, so a typical day would miss them. Three further checks follow:

  • Heat: a transformer may carry more than its rating for some hours, especially in cold weather. We compute its oil and winding temperature hour by hour with the outdoor temperature (international standard IEC 60076-7 for oil transformers) and mark a bottleneck as thermally critical when the transformer would get too hot or age faster than normal.
  • Voltage in summer: for the 24 hours with the highest feed-in we compute the voltage along the cables, separately for each way of running the batteries, once without and once with the reactive-power rule Q(U) of the Austrian connection rules.
  • Voltage in winter: for the 24 hours with the highest demand we compute the voltage drop along the cables (2030 and 2040).

From each bottleneck we derive what would fix it: either the number of home batteries needed, or how many standard sizes larger the transformer would have to be. For the batteries we simulate a fleet that covers the overload and recharges only in hours with room under the rating, counting the energy and the power of a 10 kWh / 5 kW unit with the same discounts for availability and response (and for ageing on the energy). Above 1,000 kVA a transformer needs a new medium-voltage connection.

Assumption
Hourly values: peaks shorter than an hour are smoothed out. Businesses only in the sensitivity (chapter 4). Loads with a power factor of 0.97 in the winter load flow. All three phases loaded equally.

Step 8 · Compare ways to run batteries and charge cars

Each case is simulated once with today's behaviour and once with each alternative:

Home batteries

Self-consumption
Today's behaviour; the baseline.
Fixed limit
The battery may never feed in above a fixed share; no forecast.
Grid-serving (forecast)
Using the next day's weather forecast, the battery keeps room free and charges during the midday peak instead of in the morning.
Grid-serving (night discharge)
As before, and it also empties at night so there is room the next day.
Same, simple forecast
As before, but the forecast is just “tomorrow like today”. Real day-ahead forecasts are better than this and worse than a perfect one, so the two cases bracket reality.

Electric cars

Uncontrolled
Cars charge as soon as they arrive.
Everyone at 6 pm
Stress test: after a cold day all cars plug in at the same time (“simultaneous”).
WiNAP
A cheaper winter night tariff from 23:00 (Austrian “Winter-Niedrigpreis-Phase”); many cars then start at the same moment.
Dynamic grid tariff (T3d)
Our proposal: a discount signal per grid segment tells cars when charging is cheap for the grid.
Smart charging
The grid operator shares the room under the rating among the plugged-in cars, car by car, each at most at its wallbox power; cars that leave soonest go first. What is missing at 7 am is reported as not charged.
V2G
Smart charging plus released cars giving power back while heat pumps and households alone exceed the rating: at most 11 kW and 15 kWh per car and evening, 70 % of evenings released, and only as much as can be recharged by 7 am. That recharging is counted at the transformer.
Assumption
With the perfect forecast the battery results are an upper bound; the simple forecast gives the lower bound. Because heat pumps and cars are placed at random, we repeat the main winter cases with 20 different random draws (called “seeds”) and give the spread from the 10 % to the 90 % value; the live simulation (chapter 6) does the same per run.

4 Results

Updated 24 Oct 2026 (evening) after an independent technical review: transformer heat check, batteries that recharge between bottlenecks, cars simulated one by one, a simple forecast as lower bound, owners' electricity bills, 20 random draws, sensitivity and costs.

  • Summer: batteries lower the peak, but do not replace reinforcement. How much depends on the forecast: With a perfect forecast and emptying at night, home batteries cut the feed-in peak per transformer by 13–15 % (median, three weather years). With the simple forecast “tomorrow like today” it is only 0–6 %. Real forecasts lie in between. With today's PV, 1–3 of 257 transformers have a feed-in bottleneck; with PV ×1.5 it is 19, with PV ×2 32–34.
  • It costs the owners a little. With a good forecast a battery household pays about 4–5 € a year more (median; 1 in 10 pays 23 € or more), with the simple forecast about 23–25 €. The compensation in chapter 5 covers this. A fixed limit without forecast would cost about 85–90 € a year.
  • Winter: batteries are the wrong tool. The bottleneck comes from heat pumps and lasts for days in a cold spell; between peaks there is little room to recharge. In 2040, 193–210 of 257 transformers are above their rating, depending on the weather year; about half of them (90–150) would also get too hot. These numbers depend most on how large the real transformers are (see “How robust”). In the 2040 moderate case (35 % heat pumps) 152–181 are above their rating with uncontrolled charging, but only 7–30 with smart charging (optimistic count, see the winter section): there the evening peak comes mainly from the cars.
  • Lines: the transformer comes first. Added after the pitch: in a second, separate synthetic grid (432 stations, every cable section sized and checked) the cables are the first bottleneck at only 5–6 stations in 2030 and 11–17 in 2040 with 50 % heat pumps; the transformer comes first at 46–126 and 214–227. In the main cases (2030 and 2040 with 50 % heat pumps) smart charging that respects the cables removes the remaining line overloads; in the 70 % stress case (2012) 14 stations remain (see lines).
  • Electric cars: smart charging solves every case in which heat pumps and households alone stay below the transformer rating.
  • V2G is a reserve, not a cure. At the weak rural segment in 2040 V2G cuts the overload hours from 149 to 43 a year, but the peak stays 82 kW above the rating and the cars leave with more energy missing (9.5 instead of 5.3 MWh a year), because during a cold spell there is no room to recharge them.
  • Costs: batteries pay only where few are needed. A grid-serving battery beats a larger transformer only if a segment needs no more than about 6 of them (about 14–24 if the batteries already exist and only the box is paid). Most bottlenecks need far more. The best use is short bottlenecks around 2030 with batteries already installed, and bridging the time until a transformer is replaced.

Almost never. In our synthetic low-voltage grid the transformer is the first bottleneck at almost every station that reaches a limit; the cables come first at only 5–17 of 432 stations.   • Smart charging and V2G work at the transformer. In 2030 (2012) they cut the overloaded transformers from 127 to 36 and 10. The price is energy the cars lack in a cold spell (280 MWh with smart charging, 508 MWh with V2G in 2040 with 50 % heat pumps, weather 2012).
  • Respecting the lines costs nothing extra here. Smart charging that only watches the transformer leaves 34–40 stations with a line overload, because it shares out the charging power without looking at the feeders. If it respects every cable section as well, none is left, and the energy not charged stays the same (280 MWh). In the stress case (2012) 14 stations remain instead of 52, and slightly more energy stays uncharged. What is controlled here is EV charging. That calls to home batteries should therefore be able to address one feeder, not only a whole transformer (profile, P4 and P9), is our inference; gridsim does not simulate battery calls.
  • Grid storage helps little. A battery owned by the grid operator behind the most overloaded section of a feeder (2040, 50 %, 2012): 170 such batteries with 24.8 MWh in total (146 kWh on average) lower the stations with line overload only from 101 to 88 and the overloaded cable length from 11.0 to 8.3 km; the transformers hardly change (298 → 296). With line-aware smart charging none is needed.

What these line results cannot say

  • Synthetic grid. Routes, lengths and cable types come from streets, buildings and a planning rule, not from the grid operator. Single stations can be far off; only the order of magnitude and the direction across the region are reliable. Minimum cable sizes and sizing for a 1-in-10 load make the grid rather stronger than the real one, so future line bottlenecks are rather underestimated.
  • Voltage is not evaluated. All figures are thermal (current against rated current). At 93 of 432 stations (21.5 %) 122 feeders keep the current limit but not the voltage limit even with the strongest cable; there a real grid operator would have to act because of voltage. Where voltage limits first, the thermal view underestimates the need.
  • Transformer ratings from a rule of thumb (2–3 kVA per home, rounded to standard sizes), not from the grid operator. One size larger shifts stations from “transformer first” to “line first”. The model already shows 11 overloaded transformers today in the cold year 2012, while the real ones carry today's load.
  • Today's shares are Upper Austria values. Heat pumps and EVs “today” use figures for the whole province; district figures are not published.
  • Uncontrolled charging at 11 kW is an upper bound. Cars charge at full wallbox power after arriving in the evening. Salzburg Netz measures about 0.5 kW extra load per vehicle at large transformer stations (99 % quantile, Maier/Groiß, EnInnov 2026), so the EV bottlenecks are an upper bound.

Load flow check: the simple summation along the feeders is 2.5 % (today) and 3.9 % (2040, 50 %) below a full pandapower load flow (median). Rated currents: Weisenstein et al. 2022. Full acceptance report with all settings: docs/gridsim-leitungen-abnahme.md in the project repository.

Electric cars on a winter evening: smart charging and V2G

Smart charging is the strongest lever for electric cars: it solves every case except where heat pumps alone already exceed the transformer. V2G then only shortens the overload, and the cars leave with less charge.

Costs: batteries, larger transformers or curtailment?

Batteries pay off only where a few are enough: short peaks, batteries that are already installed, or as a bridge until the transformer is replaced. For long bottlenecks a larger transformer or some curtailment is cheaper.

For every transformer with a bottleneck we compared the yearly cost of three remedies: a larger transformer (spread over 40 years), grid-serving batteries (box and extra subsidy spread over 15 years, operation, compensation per shifted kWh) and, in summer, curtailing PV (value of the lost energy).

The 2,000 € extra subsidy is below our tiered proposal in chapter 5: at tier 2, a 10 kWh battery for 6,500 € would get about 5,200 € instead of 1,500 € today, about 3,700 € extra. With that, batteries would win at even fewer segments.

5 Our proposal: borrow batteries for short peaks

The grid operator borrows home batteries for short periods with a signed request, and the household never loses money.

The results say where this helps: against short peaks, where batteries are already installed, and as a bridge until a transformer is replaced. For long-lasting bottlenecks a larger transformer or some curtailment is cheaper (chapter 4, costs). Where batteries help, they are already in place, but they run for self-consumption and are often full by late morning, just before the peak. Nobody can ask them to wait, because there is no standard way to ask: Austrian law requires a control interface for solar systems since 1 June 2026 (§ 76 of the Electricity Act ElWG), but not for batteries, and every manufacturer uses its own protocol, often via its cloud. And because thousands of batteries switching at once could harm the grid as easily as help it, every request must be protected.

New terms for this chapter

Signed
Carries a digital signature: a cryptographic proof of who sent a message and that nobody changed it. Each sender has its own key and certificate.
The box
A small control unit next to the electricity meter that receives requests, checks them and controls the battery. Its energy manager is the software that decides when the battery charges and discharges.
Call, test call
A call is a request from the grid operator to the batteries of one segment. A test call is a short, regular trial run that measures how reliably they respond.
Smart meter
The digital electricity meter that records consumption and feed-in every 15 minutes.
LoRaWAN, OpenADR
LoRaWAN is a low-power radio network that reaches many devices at once without internet. OpenADR is an existing internet standard for sending such requests.
Day-ahead price
The electricity price for each quarter hour of tomorrow, set at the power exchange (EPEX) the day before.
IES, interoperability profile
IES (Integrating the Energy System) is a method to describe who exchanges which information in an energy process, so devices from different manufacturers work together. The result is an interoperability profile.
  1. BoxModular, certified, LoRaWAN by default. Checks every command itself.
  2. Signed callOne message per segment, with limits and expiry. No manufacturer cloud in the path.
  3. Test callsShort, regular, measured: how much power can the grid operator rely on?
  4. VerificationSmart meter and the box's own meter confirm what really happened.
  5. Subsidy and refundMore control allowed, more subsidy. Any extra cost from a call is refunded.

Scope: designed for the EU, piloted in Austria with the Steyr region as demo area.

Conformance proposal: every box and every grid-operator system is tested against published test messages (valid, expired, replayed, wrong segment, wrong role, over the limits) at a joint test event. Only the valid message may cause an action. Inverter interfaces are tested against the minimum values S1–S5 and R1–R5 of profile v2.

Priority inside the box

  1. Safety and fallback (battery protection, temperature)
  2. Signed call of the grid operator
  3. Minimum reserve kept for calls
  4. The household's own preferences
  5. Self-consumption

The household sets eight preferences (price thresholds for charging and feed-in, backup reserve for power cuts, maximum power, charging from the grid on/off, trading on price differences on/off, minimum price difference, paid discharge calls on/off). Calls may override some of them for a limited time and always show source, reason, start and end. The backup reserve is never overridden.

Security rules

  • Every command is checked in the box: signature, role, expiry, sequence number, limits.
  • Prices outside −50 … +100 ct/kWh are rejected. A fake price via Home Assistant has no effect, because prices only arrive signed via the box.
  • Home Assistant connects through its own channel only, with no route to the core module or the grid operator's channel.
  • Modules run signed software and talk to the core over an authenticated internal bus, as in cars. Unapproved modules are switched off.
  • Random start delays and staggering within a segment prevent the box from creating a new peak.

Compensation: the customer is never worse off

Compensation = max(0, extra purchase cost − extra feed-in revenue) + wear allowance × energy shifted, plus a fee for the call. The comparison is what the box's own energy manager would have done without the call, using the household's preferences from 24 hours before (so nobody can game it). decided principle; wear allowance 5 ct/kWh proposal; fee level open.

The box

Modules on a standard mounting rail next to the meter: power supply; a core module (microcontroller with a security chip, the only part that creates commands for the battery); four identical radio and network slots (LoRaWAN as standard, mobile radio or Ethernet for Home Assistant optional); meter interface; wired connections to the battery's inverter; optional own signed meter. Any manufacturer may build modules; only the trusted authority may approve them.

Fit with existing rules

  • Builds on § 76 ElWG (control interface for solar systems) and closes the gap for batteries through the subsidy condition.
  • The Austrian connection rules for small generators (TOR Typ A V1.4, valid from 1 June 2026, chapter 5.4.1) require a direct line from the grid operator to a digital interface in the customer's installation, using OpenADR (the Austrian protocol is due by the end of 2026), no third-party systems such as manufacturer clouds in between, the target power within one minute, and a fallback to 70 % of the module peak power if communication fails. They have no battery setpoint. Our box can serve as that interface; LoRaWAN as an extra broadcast channel needs a justification in the full profile.
  • proposal The dynamic grid tariff per segment (T3d, discount only, step 8) as a voluntary first step before signed calls. Grid tariffs are set today per grid level and area by regulation; a tariff per transformer would need a change to that regulation.

What a pilot still needs

The hackathon profile shows the idea. A grid operator needs more before testing it on real homes:

  • Supplier and balance group. The balance group is the supplier's account in which bought and sold energy must match every quarter hour. Each call moves energy away from the supplier's plan, so the supplier is told in advance and paid back afterwards. Batteries already sold to other flexibility markets need clear rules for when the grid operator's call comes first.
  • Fixed limits in the box. Limits that no signed message can change cap how often, how long and how fast batteries react (for example at most 3 calls and 4 hours a day). At the end of a call and after a radio outage, batteries return to normal operation spread over about fifteen minutes rather than all at once. A stolen key or a radio outage therefore cannot cause a sudden jump on the grid.
  • Radio only for broadcasts. LoRaWAN carries only short broadcasts such as prices and calls: a signed price list needs four radio packets and about 11 seconds of airtime at the slowest setting. It cannot carry confirmations or meter readings from thousands of homes, so results are checked with the existing smart meters.
  • Fair compensation, checked by the grid operator. The grid operator runs the published comparison calculation, not the box, and checks it against smart-meter data and comparison households.
  • Interfaces and keys. A minimum battery-inverter interface using one of the open standards SunSpec, EEBUS or IEEE 2030.5, certificates per role with a way to revoke them over the radio, and signed device software (firmware) that cannot be swapped back to an older, weaker version.
  • A first pilot in Steyr would cover two or three transformer areas with 30 to 60 homes for one solar season.

Details: interoperability profile v2 in the project repository (actors, processes P7–P10, limits, certificate system, LoRaWAN airtime, pilot plan).

    7 All assumptions and data sources at a glance

    Why the simulation is built this way

    The simulation answers one question from the grid operator's side: at which transformer, in which hour, would grid-serving batteries replace grid reinforcement, and how many would it take? Every choice follows from that question and from the rule that only open data may be used.

    ChoiceWhyLimit
    All 257 transformers, not one example grid Calls address a segment. The grid operator needs to know where batteries help and where they do not; one example grid hides the spread between a weak rural and a strong urban transformer. Where map data has no transformer, one is placed synthetically.
    Synthetic low-voltage grids (step 2) The real grid is critical infrastructure and not public. Calibrated against published Austrian field grids. Results per transformer are plausible, not exact. The 30 kWp threshold is an assumption.
    Cable sections checked in a second synthetic grid (432 stations, chapter 4) A call per transformer is not enough if a single feeder is the bottleneck. The detailed grid shows how often that happens. Thermal only, voltage not evaluated (93 of 432 stations have voltage-only cases); synthetic; transformer ratings from a rule of thumb.
    Every hour of a full year (step 7) Bottlenecks are rare events. A typical day or a monthly mean would miss them, and the battery need depends on how long each event lasts. Hourly, not 15-minute values: short peaks are smoothed. The live simulation uses 15-minute steps for the single day.
    Three real weather years (step 6) Summer feed-in and winter demand both depend on the weather; the spread shows how robust a result is. One temperature per transformer.
    Transformer first, voltage for the 24 hardest hours (step 7) The transformer rating is the limit a call protects, so it is checked every hour. Voltage is the first limit in rural grids, so it is checked where feed-in is highest. This keeps 257 transformers × 8,760 hours × all scenarios computable. Reactive power Q(U) checked as a variant in summer; winter voltage drop for 2030 and 2040; voltage outside these hours not checked; all phases equal.
    Heat check of each transformer (step 7) A transformer may carry more than its rating for some hours, especially in the cold. Only a thermally critical bottleneck is urgent. Typical values for oil distribution transformers; the station housing is assumed 5 K warmer than outside.
    20 random draws and one-at-a-time sensitivity (chapter 4) Shows which results depend on chance and which on a single assumption. Assumptions are varied one at a time, not all together.
    Both directions: summer feed-in and winter demand The proposal uses batteries both ways. Simulating both shows where they help (summer) and where they honestly do not (cold spell). Businesses only as a sensitivity; heat pumps never switched off.
    Today's self-consumption as the baseline (step 8) A subsidy condition is only fair if it barely costs the owner. The electricity bill shows that: about +4–5 € a year with a good forecast, +23–25 € with a simple one, +85–90 € with a fixed limit. Perfect and simple forecast bracket reality; prices 25 ct (purchase) and 7 ct (feed-in) per kWh are assumptions.
    Need counted in 10 kWh home batteries (step 7) The number of batteries per segment is what subsidy and test calls are planned against. In summer the energy is the binding limit; for short winter peaks in 2030 the power is, at about half of the transformers. Battery shares (step 4) are assumptions.
    Scenarios PV ×1.5 / ×2, heat pumps and EVs 2030 / 2040 (step 5) Today only 1–3 of 257 transformers have a feed-in bottleneck (partly because today's PV is part of our sizing). The interface and the subsidy are meant for the build-out. Renovation until 2040 only as a sensitivity; heat demand calibrated to today's census values.
    Mocked batteries and test calls There is no field data on how fast and reliably home batteries respond to a grid operator. The mock shows the loop the profile requires. Manufacturer groups A–E are invented; response times are illustrative.
