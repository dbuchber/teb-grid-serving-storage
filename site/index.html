TEB Hackathon · Challenge Grid-Serving Storage

Can the batteries in our basements relieve the local grid?

A proposal for how a grid operator can borrow customers’ batteries safely, tested by simulating all 257 transformers of our test region Steyr, Upper Austria.

The problem
up to 193 of 257

Up to 193 of 257 transformers in our test region could be overloaded in winter 2040.

A transformer is the station in a neighbourhood that supplies the houses around it. Its rating is the power it may carry continuously. If 70 % of buildings heat with a heat pump and 60 % of cars are electric, most transformers go above their rating, even in a mild winter like 2023. This is the stress case and an upper bound: if the real transformers are as large as the biggest in published field grids, it is about 100. In summer the opposite happens: with twice today’s solar, 32–34 transformers receive more power from the roofs than they can carry.

With 35 % heat pumps it is still 152–181, but smart charging of the cars brings it down to 7–30, depending on the weather year (an optimistic count).

Added after the pitch, on a separate synthetic grid (432 stations, estimated transformer ratings, uncontrolled charging): where a limit is reached it is almost always the transformer first, at 46–126 stations in 2030 and 214–227 in 2040 (50 % heat pumps); the line comes first at only 5–6 and 11–17.

Who is affected

Two parties, one battery in the basement.

The grid operator

Runs the local power lines. Against an overload it can replace a transformer with a larger one, which is expensive and takes years, or limit solar systems, which wastes energy.

“Can I use the batteries that are already there, for a few hours?”

The household

Owns a home battery next to its solar system. Today it charges with the household's own solar power and empties in the evening. The owner decides how it runs.

“What does it cost me, and does it wear out my battery?”

    Our proposal

    Borrow batteries for short peaks, with a signed request, and the household never loses money.

    The owner runs the battery as usual. Only in exceptional situations, and for a limited time, may the grid operator ask all batteries behind one transformer to charge or discharge, so that solar systems and consumers do not have to be cut back (curtailed).

    1. The boxA small control unit next to the electricity meter. It receives requests, checks them and controls the battery.
    2. A signed callThe grid operator asks all batteries behind one transformer at once. A digital signature proves who sent it; limits and an expiry are built in.
    3. Test callsShort, regular trial runs measure how much power the grid operator can really rely on.
    4. Checked by the meterThe digital electricity meter (smart meter) confirms what really happened.
    5. Subsidy and refundMore control allowed, more subsidy. Any extra cost from a call is paid back.

    Where it helps: short peaks, where batteries are already installed, and as a bridge until a transformer is replaced. In a long cold spell batteries cannot recharge; there smart charging and larger transformers do the work.

    Who exchanges what, in twelve processes.

    IES (Integrating the Energy System) describes who takes part in an energy process and which information they exchange, so devices from different manufacturers work together. A rough, reusable profile, not a full specification.

    • Grid operator
    • Box + household
    • Battery inverter
    • Supplier
    • Price authority (E-Control)
    • Trusted authority
    • Subsidy body
    • Aggregator

    Set up

    P1 Commissioning: certified modules, first test call; first half of the subsidy is paid.

    P9 Segment: the grid operator assigns the box to its transformer and feeder.

    Every day

    P2 Prices: one signed price list for tomorrow, 96 quarter hours, broadcast to all boxes.

    P3 Test call: short trial, e.g. once a month; measures what the grid operator can rely on.

    Bottleneck

    P4 Call: one signed command per transformer or feeder: charge or discharge, power, start, maximum duration, expiry, random start delay.

    P7 Supplier is told in advance. P8: third-party marketers pause for that segment.

    Afterwards

    P5 Verification: the grid operator checks smart-meter data against what the battery would have done without the call (open reference algorithm); extra cost refunded plus a fee.

    P6 Fallback to self-consumption. P10: keys revoked and replaced over the air.

    Interfaces: grid operator → box: OpenADR (required for solar systems by the Austrian connection rules; for batteries our proposal) and a LoRaWAN radio broadcast · box → inverter: SunSpec, EEBUS or IEEE 2030.5 · verification: smart meter.

    Three storage classes, same processes: home (up to 30 kW) and business behind the meter (30–250 kW) relieve the street transformer, via the box or a certified building energy manager. Large storage (from 250 kW) relieves the medium-voltage grid and the substation, controlled by telecontrol with acknowledgement, a day-ahead flexibility schedule (P11) and flexible grid access (P12). Business and large storage are not yet in the simulation.

    Security, resilience and what is real

    Thousands of batteries switching at once could harm the grid as easily as help it.

    What we protect against

    • Forged or replayed command: the box checks signature, role, expiry, sequence number and limits itself.
    • Stolen key: fixed limits in the box that no message can lift (e.g. at most 3 calls and 4 hours a day); keys revoked over the radio.
    • Radio outage, all at once: random start delay, return to normal spread over about 15 minutes, fallback to self-consumption.
    • Fake prices from the home network: prices only arrive signed via the box.

    Where AI is used

    We trained an image-recognition model on aerial photos to find solar systems built since 2023: it finds 55 % of real systems, 69 % of its finds are real. We compared its results with the BOKU map roof by roof. The simulation itself uses the BOKU map; the model result is shown on the map only. No AI decides in the call process: calls are deterministic, signed and checkable.

    Test region

    Steyr in Upper Austria is the region we chose to test the method. Everything runs the same for any other region with open data.

    What it takes

    A pilot in Steyr: 2–3 transformer areas, 30–60 homes, one solar season.

    • The supplier is told and paid back for every call that moves energy away from its plan.
    • Fixed limits in the box that no message can change: how often, how long, how fast batteries react.
    • One open interface for battery inverters and keys per role that can be revoked.
    • Fair compensation, checked by the grid operator against smart-meter data.
    • A forecast per transformer: a machine-learning model that predicts tomorrow's load and feed-in of each transformer, so the grid operator knows in advance where a call is needed.
